Every creator asking "how do I get more subscribers" is really asking two questions, and the second one matters more. The first is *where do people come from*. The second, quieter one is *what does each of those places cost me* — not in money, but in time, control, and the kind of subscriber it sends. This guide sits inside our [growth hub](/resources/growth), and it is a reality check rather than a ranking: there is no single best channel, only tradeoffs you choose on purpose.

Before the channel-by-channel walk, hold two ideas in your head, because between them they explain almost every result you will ever see.

## Rented land versus owned land

Most acquisition happens on someone else's platform, under someone else's rules, ranked by someone else's algorithm. That is rented land. It can be astonishingly productive, and it can vanish overnight when a rule changes or an account gets limited. Owned land — a contact list you control, a fanbase that follows you by name across platforms — is slower to build and far harder to take away. Nearly every channel below is rented. The strategic move is to use rented reach to build something you own, so that a single suspension is a setback instead of an extinction event.

## Volume versus intent

The other axis is who actually shows up. Some channels deliver a flood of loosely-curious viewers; others deliver a trickle of people already reaching for their wallet. High-volume, low-intent traffic looks great in your view counts and disappoints at the paywall. Low-volume, high-intent traffic looks quiet and converts. Neither is "better." They fail in different ways, and they demand different follow-through once someone subscribes — which is where [why subscribers cancel](/resources/growth/why-subscribers-cancel) picks up the thread.

## Effort now versus effort forever

There is a third axis worth naming, because it decides what you can actually sustain. Some channels charge you once and keep paying out — a piece of content that keeps surfacing, a community relationship that keeps sending people. Others charge you every single day and stop the moment you do: the feed that has to be fed, the promotion that dies when the spend ends. Neither is wrong, but they demand honesty about your own capacity. A channel that only works while you are actively feeding it is a job, not an asset, and stacking too many of those is how creators burn out with nothing that compounds. Weigh each channel not just by what it returns, but by whether the effort it asks is a one-time deposit or a standing bill.

## Reddit: high intent, high rules

Reddit sends some of the most motivated traffic there is, because people are browsing a specific community for exactly the thing you make. The trade is that every subreddit is its own country with its own laws, moderation is unforgiving, and the platform punishes anything that reads as spam. It rewards creators who behave like members rather than billboards. The upside is durable and close to free; the downside is that one careless week can cost you the accounts you spent months building. We gave it a full operational playbook — [Reddit without getting banned](/resources/growth/reddit-without-getting-banned) — because the details are exactly where people fall down.

## Short-form video: reach without ownership

TikTok, Reels and Shorts are the widest top-of-funnel available anywhere. The algorithm can put an unknown creator in front of an enormous audience with no follower base at all, which is intoxicating and genuinely powerful. The costs are three. The content is demanding to produce consistently, week after week. The audience is low-intent by default — they came to be entertained, not to buy. And these platforms are the strictest about anything adult-adjacent, so the line you walk to stay visible is narrow and moves without warning. Short-form is a reach engine, not a closing tool. It works when it feeds something that actually converts, and it wastes you when it is the whole plan.

## Open feeds: personality that compounds

Open text-and-image feeds sit in a useful middle. They tolerate more than the video platforms, they let you post frequently, and they let a personality compound over time. The catch is that reach is uneven and increasingly tied to how the platform feels about your account on any given day, and organic discovery can stay shallow without either paid amplification or relentless consistency. Treated as a place to build a recognizable voice and a repeatable posting habit, an open feed earns its keep. Treated as a slot machine you pull for a viral moment, it quietly drains the hours you do not have.

## Paid traffic: fast, honest, unforgiving

Paid promotion — buying placement, or paying another account to send its audience — is the most controllable channel by far. You decide the spend, the timing and the scale, and you can turn it off in an instant. That control is exactly why it is unforgiving: it converts your assumptions into results immediately, and if your funnel does not hold water, paid traffic simply shows you that faster and at cost. It rewards operators who already know their numbers cold and punishes anyone guessing. The rule is boring and it holds: never scale paid until the free version of the same funnel is already, quietly, working.

## Cross-promotion and shoutouts

Trading exposure with other creators — shoutout-for-shoutout, paid shoutouts, or genuine collaborations — is one of the oldest and best-fitting channels, because the audience you borrow is already made of people who subscribe to creators. Intent is high and the context does much of the selling for you. The tradeoffs are trust and fit: a shoutout from a mismatched or low-quality partner converts poorly and can dent your credibility, and paid shoutout markets are full of inflated and fake audiences. This channel lives or dies on partner selection, which is judgment rather than a tactic you can automate or buy your way through.

## Aggregators, directories and "free" link farms

There is a whole tier of link aggregators, "free trial" directories and mass-DM schemes that promise volume. Be skeptical. The traffic is low-intent at best and fake at worst, it rarely rebills, and some of it carries real account risk. Occasionally a legitimate directory fits a specific niche, but as a category this is where creators pour effort into numbers that never become a durable base. Volume that does not renew is not growth; it is a treadmill, and you feel it most in your [rebills and renewals](/resources/growth/rebills-and-renewals).

## The portfolio, not the silver bullet

Put the channels side by side and the pattern is clear. The high-intent channels — Reddit, cross-promotion — are durable but capped by how much of that attention exists. The high-volume channels — short-form, open feeds — are uncapped but low-intent and platform-dependent. Paid is the amplifier that turns a working funnel into a bigger one and a broken funnel into a faster loss. No single one of these is a business. A deliberate mix is: one or two reach engines feeding a couple of high-intent closers, with everything pointed at something you own.

The mistake we see most is chasing the channel that worked for someone else instead of the one that fits your content, your risk tolerance and the time you can sustain. The second mistake is judging channels by traffic instead of by what happens after someone subscribes. A channel is only as good as its retained subscriber, so read every acquisition number next to your churn and your rebill base, and let your [weekly reporting](/resources/growth/weekly-reporting) tell you which sources are sending subscribers who stay. Start there, keep the mix small enough to maintain, and revisit it on a schedule instead of on a whim. The rest of the [growth hub](/resources/growth) is built around that loop.
