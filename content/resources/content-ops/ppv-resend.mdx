"Resend after twenty-four hours" is one of those rules that circulates because it is simple, not because it is right. It treats resending a PPV as a mechanical timer — wait a day, send it again — when resending is actually one of the sharper judgement calls in the inbox, because it cuts both ways. Done to the right people it is close to free money; done to the wrong people it is a way to annoy your best fans into muting you. This guide is part of our [content operations hub](/resources/content-ops), and it is about the decision rule that replaces the timer.

## A resend is a coin with two sides

The reason there is no simple timer answer is that a resend does two opposite things depending on who receives it. To a fan who **missed** the first send — never saw it, scrolled past, was asleep — a resend is a legitimate second chance at a sale you would otherwise have lost, and it can be pure upside. To a fan who **saw** it and chose not to buy, the same resend is you asking again for something they already declined, which reads as not listening and costs you goodwill. Same message, same content, opposite effect — and the only thing that changed is the state of the person receiving it.

That is why time-since-send, the variable the folklore fixates on, is the least useful one in the decision. Whether an hour or a week has passed tells you almost nothing about whether a given fan missed the send or passed on it, and missed-versus-passed is the entire question. Resending well is not about the clock; it is about reading the recipient.

## The variables that actually decide it

A useful resend rule runs on the state of the person, not the age of the message:

- **Did they open it?** This is the single most important split, because it cleanly separates *missed it* from *passed on it*. Someone who never opened the first send is a candidate for a genuine second chance. Someone who opened it and did not buy has already decided, and resending the same thing pesters them.
- **Are they worth the spend?** A resend costs attention, so aim it where the return justifies it — a high-value fan who plausibly missed a send is worth a careful second touch; a low-intent segment that rarely buys is exactly where resends turn into spam. Spend your resends where buyers actually are.
- **Why didn't they buy?** Missed it is a resend. Saw it and passed is not — at least not the same message. A price objection is not fixed by sending the price again. Matching the resend to the reason is the difference between a nudge and a nuisance.
- **How recently have you last hit them — at all?** Not time since *this* send, but time since your *last* message to this person, because that is what governs fatigue. A fan you messaged twice already this week does not need a third touch no matter how good the PPV is.

Run the decision through those, and "should I resend?" stops being a reflex and becomes an answerable question with a different answer for different people.

## The rule, stated simply

Resend to the people who plausibly **missed** it — did not open, worth the spend, not already fatigued — and leave alone the people who **saw** it and passed. And on the rare occasion you do want another shot at the people who passed, **change something real**: the preview, the framing, the angle, or the offer. Nobody buys the identical thing they already declined just because it came twice, but a person who passed on one version may well buy a better one. An identical resend to someone who already opened it is not a strategy; it is an irritation with a send button.

## Count the fatigue, every time

The trap under all of this is treating a resend as costless because the content already exists. It is not costless — it spends the list's attention, and a resend aimed badly spends it for nothing while nudging people toward the mute button. A resend that books a sale today but tires out a segment can be net-negative once you count the next few sends it dampened, which is the exact same fatigue logic that governs [over-blasting the list](/resources/content-ops/mass-message-checklist) and [running discounts too often](/resources/pricing/discount-guardrails). Price the resend the way you price any send — its [break-even](/resources/pricing/ppv-break-even) includes the labour and the fatigue, not just the extra buyers — and it should clear that bar before it goes.

Resend deliberately, to the right people, with something changed, under a fatigue ceiling, and the resend becomes what it should be: a quiet recovery of sales you nearly missed, rather than a slow way to teach your best fans to tune you out.
