Creators pour their effort into the content behind the lock — the shoot, the edit, the thing they are actually selling — and then send it out behind a preview they gave thirty seconds of thought to. It is exactly backwards, because at the moment a fan decides whether to buy, the content behind the lock does not exist to them. The only thing they can see is the preview, and the preview is therefore the entire pitch. This guide is part of our [content operations hub](/resources/content-ops), and it is about treating the preview as what it actually is: the product, at the point of sale.

## The buyer only ever sees the preview

Hold this plainly, because everything follows from it: **a fan buys a PPV from the preview, not the content.** The teaser frame and the visible slice of the caption are all the information they have when they decide. The content itself — however good — has no vote in the sale, because they cannot see it until after they have paid. It affects whether they are satisfied afterward, and satisfaction matters for the next sale. But *this* sale is won or lost entirely on the preview.

This reframes where your attention should go. A stunning piece of content behind a flat, badly-cropped preview goes unbought, and unbought content earns nothing no matter how good it is. A merely-good piece behind a preview that lands sells. So at the point of sale, the preview is not the advertisement for the product — it *is* the product, the only part of it the buyer can evaluate, and it deserves a real share of the effort the content got.

## What the preview has to do, and how long it has to do it

The preview does its job in brutal conditions. It arrives in a crowded inbox, often mid-scroll, competing with everything else, and it gets a fraction of a second of real attention. In that fraction of a second it has to communicate desire — make the fan feel what they are missing and believe that paying resolves it. There is no time for a slow build; the preview either lands immediately or it is scrolled past, and a scrolled-past PPV is a lost sale that never registers as one.

That time pressure is why the small, mechanical details of a preview matter so much more than they seem to. There is no margin for a preview that needs a second look to make sense, because it will not get one. Everything below is about making the preview win its fraction of a second.

## The pre-send check

Before any PPV goes out, run it against the same short list. It takes less time than the send is worth losing.

- **Framing and crop.** The preview is usually shown cropped, and often not the way you framed it. Confirm the most compelling part of the image is actually inside the crop the fan will see — not just centered in your full-size editor. A selling point cropped out of the preview might as well not exist.
- **The first moment of motion.** For video, the preview frequently opens on the first frame or a brief auto-play, so the opening instant is the whole trailer. If the video opens on a dead beat — a static setup, a nothing frame — it dies there. Lead with the strongest moment, not the chronological first one.
- **The tease level.** Enough to create desire, not so much that there is no reason to pay. Over-revealing gives the sale away; under-revealing leaves no desire to convert. This is the judgement call at the heart of a preview, and it is worth getting deliberately right rather than by accident.
- **The caption up to the cut.** The caption truncates in the inbox, usually sooner than you expect. Read only the part that shows before the cut-off and ask whether *that* sells. A brilliant caption whose hook lands after the truncation is a brilliant caption the fan never reads.

None of these are about the content. All of them decide whether the content ever gets bought.

## Where previews usually fail

The common failures are consistent enough to watch for by name: the best moment buried past where the preview shows; a video that opens on a static, lifeless first frame; a caption whose hook is cut off by truncation; a crop that hides the exact thing that would have sold it; and the two opposite sins of revealing so much there is no reason to pay, or so little there is no desire to. Almost every underperforming send is one of these — and almost none of them are content problems. They are preview problems wearing the content's clothes, which is why creators misdiagnose them as "the content didn't land" and reshoot when they should have re-previewed.

## Test it the way the fan actually sees it

Here is the discipline that catches most of it: **preview your send the way the fan receives it, not the way you made it.** You see the PPV at full size, in your editor, with the entire caption visible and all the context in your head. The fan sees a cropped thumbnail, a truncated caption, at inbox size, with none of your context, probably mid-scroll. Those are two completely different experiences of the same send, and the gap between them is where sales leak.

So check it in the real send interface, at the real size, with the caption cut where the inbox cuts it — as a stranger scrolling past, not as the creator who knows what everything means. If you cannot bring yourself to look at it that coldly, the preview is not ready, because the fan will look at it exactly that coldly and give it exactly that fraction of a second.

## Where the preview sits in the operation

The preview is the **offer stage** of your messaging — the moment the [sequence](/resources/content-ops/scripts-dont-sell-sequences-do) actually asks for the sale — so a stronger preview makes the whole sequence convert better without changing a word of the conversation around it. It also moves the math directly: because a better-converting preview lifts the buyers on every send, it improves the break-even on all of them, which is the arithmetic in [what a PPV has to earn](/resources/pricing/ppv-break-even). And the best previews are not rescued after the fact — they are planned at the shot list, with the selling frame decided while you are shooting rather than hunted for afterward, which is why the preview belongs in [batching](/resources/content-ops/how-batching-actually-works) and not in a panic before the send. Treat the preview as the product it is, check it every time before it goes, and the same content starts earning what it was always capable of. **This is general operational information, not a guarantee of results.** How a preview performs depends on your content, your audience and factors outside anyone's control. Treat this as a framework to adapt, not a promise about your sales or income.
