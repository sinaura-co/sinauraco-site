Most advice on picking an agency is about reading people — the vibe, the promises, how the call feels. This guide is about something duller and far more reliable: whether the agency is a business you can actually find. That is due diligence you can do in an afternoon, from your couch, and almost nobody does it.

It belongs at the front of [choosing a management agency](/resources/agency-selection), not the end, because it is the cheapest test you will run and the hardest for a bad actor to fake. A slick pitch is easy to produce. A real registered entity, at a real address, with real people and its own domain, sitting under a jurisdiction where you have recourse — that is expensive to fake and simple to check.

## It is a registered legal entity you can find by name

A real company is registered somewhere. In the US, businesses are formed at the state level, so an American agency appears in the business-entity records of the state where it filed — searchable, usually free, on the Secretary of State's site or its equivalent. You look up the legal name and the entity is there: when it was formed, its status, whether it is in good standing.

What you are checking is not the detail of the record but that one exists at all. If you take the agency's legal name and cannot find it in any registry, anywhere, that absence is not a paperwork gap. It is the finding. A management company that has taken no legal form is not a company you can hold to anything.

## It has a real business address on record

A registered company has an address attached to its filing. It does not have to be a glass tower — a registered-agent address or a formation-service address counts, and many legitimate businesses use one. The point is that something on record ties the company to a physical place and a jurisdiction.

What you are looking for is the difference between an address and a handle. "DM us on the app" is not an address. An agency whose entire presence is a username, with nothing on paper connecting it to anywhere you could send a letter or serve a notice, has arranged to be unreachable by design.

## There are named humans who stand behind it

Somewhere behind a real agency are real people who put their names to it. Filings often list a registered agent, an organizer, or officers, and the people running the business tend to have professional footprints you can find. You are not hunting for anyone's home address. You are checking that the business is willing to be associated with humans at all.

An agency that hides every person behind an anonymous brand is asking for a trust it will not return. It wants your real name on a contract, your real bank account for payouts, your real content — while it stays a logo. That asymmetry is the tell.

## It contacts you from its own domain

This one takes seconds. A business that owns its identity emails you from its own domain — name@theiragency.com — not from a free account. Standing up a domain and a mailbox on it is a low bar, which is exactly why it is a useful filter: a self-described agency reachable only at a free Gmail, or only through a personal messaging app, has not cleared even that.

It is not proof of quality on its own. But paired with an entity you cannot find and an address that does not exist, a free-email "agency" is showing you what it is.

## Jurisdiction decides what recourse you actually have

The last check is the one people skip, and it is where the real risk hides. Where an agency is legally based determines what you can do if the relationship goes wrong. An agency formed in your own country sits under laws and courts you can actually reach. An anonymous operation run from somewhere you cannot identify, through an account with no legal footprint, leaves you with a business relationship and no way to enforce it.

It does not have to be your exact state. It does have to be somewhere you know. Ask directly where the entity is formed, and listen to the shape of the answer. "Somewhere overseas, we'd rather not say" is not a missing detail. It is the answer.

## Do the whole thing in an afternoon

Here is the check, start to finish, in the order to run it:

- **Get the legal name.** Not the brand, not the handle — the exact registered entity. It usually sits in the site footer, the terms page, or the draft contract. If nobody will give you a legal name to look up, stop there.
- **Search the registry.** Take that name to the business-entity search run by the state or country where the agency says it is formed. Most US states run a free public lookup. Confirm the entity exists and is active, not dissolved.
- **Read the address and the people.** Note the address on the filing — a registered-agent address is fine — and any named agent or officers. Cross-check those people against their professional footprints.
- **Check the domain.** Confirm the agency emails from its own domain, not a free account, and that the domain matches the business you are looking at.
- **Place the jurisdiction.** Note where it is formed and ask yourself, honestly, what recourse you would have from where you sit.

None of this needs a lawyer or a paid service. It needs an afternoon and a willingness to stay unimpressed by a good pitch until the boring facts check out. Run it before the conversation in [the questions to ask](/resources/agency-selection/questions-to-ask), and before you let anyone near your login or your payouts — which is its own subject in [account access and payouts](/resources/agency-selection/account-access-and-payouts).

## What a failed check actually means

A clean result here does not make an agency good. It makes it real — a business that exists, that you can find, that sits somewhere you could reach it. That is the floor, not the ceiling; everything else in this cluster is what gets built on top of it.

A failed check is different. An agency you cannot find in any registry, at no address, behind no names, on a free email account, based somewhere it will not say — that is not a business with incomplete filings. It is an operation that has arranged to be hard to hold accountable, and it usually did so on purpose. Put a guaranteed income or a promise you will never get banned on top of that, and you are not looking at a business at all; you are looking at a pitch. Both promises are red flags in their own right, because no one can keep them.
