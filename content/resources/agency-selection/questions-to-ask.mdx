Before you sign with a management agency, you will have one conversation that decides most of what follows. Not the pitch — the part where you ask direct questions and listen to how they land. This guide is a companion to [choosing a management agency](/resources/agency-selection), and it is about the questions worth asking out loud, and what a real answer sounds like next to a rehearsed one.

Here is the part most people miss: you learn more from *how* an agency answers than from the answer itself. Anyone can say the right words. What you are watching for is whether the reply is specific and repeatable, or whether it slides into reassurance the moment you ask for detail. Specifics you can hold them to are the signal. A warm deflection is the tell.

## Ask what the fee is — and what it is charged on

Start with money, because it is where the most gets lost quietly. Ask two things together: what is the fee, and is it charged on gross or on net? Those are different questions, and the second one moves more money than the first — the same share of the whole amount a subscriber pays is a bigger bite than the same share of what actually reaches you. We walk through why in [gross vs net](/resources/pricing/gross-vs-net).

A straight answer is a clear figure, a clear basis, and an offer to put both in the agreement before you sign. You do not need a rate posted on a website or a billboard; you need the fee *and its basis* written down, in your contract, where it will still be true after the handshake. A dodge sounds like a range that never resolves, a "don't worry about the percentage, focus on the growth," or a flash of irritation that you asked at all. The number and its basis should read the same in the pitch as on paper.

## Ask exactly what they will do, and how often

"Full management" is not a scope; it is a mood. Make them convert it into a list. What, specifically, will they handle — scheduling, marketing, analytics, subscriber messaging, retention, something else? And how often — what is the cadence, and what stays your job?

A straight answer is a concrete list and a rhythm you could write on a calendar. A dodge is "we handle everything," "all of it," "you won't have to think about a thing." Vague scope is how comprehensive-sounding management quietly becomes a few posts a week. The more precisely an agency can describe the work, the more likely the work actually exists.

## Ask how you leave — and read the contract that says so

Ask how the relationship ends before it begins. How much notice to exit, what happens to work in progress, how your access is returned, and whether they keep taking a share of your earnings after you are gone. Then read the agreement that answers it, [clause by clause](/resources/agency-selection/reading-the-contract) — the exit terms are where a confident business and a trap look most different on paper.

A straight answer is a stated notice period and a clean handover, offered without flinching. Our own posture is a defined notice period to leave and your access returned to you — something you should read in the agreement rather than take on our word. A dodge sounds like "why are you thinking about leaving before you've started?" A contract engineered to make leaving painful tells you exactly how confident the agency is that you would want to stay.

## Ask whether they will ever need your password

This one has a right answer, and it is short. Ask: will you ever need my password? A legitimate agency works through the platform's own manager or delegated access — roles you grant and can revoke — and never needs your login. That is precisely what the feature exists for. More on the mechanics in [account access and payouts](/resources/agency-selection/account-access-and-payouts).

A straight answer is "no — we use the platform's manager access, never your password." Any version of "it's just easier if we have your login" is a structural problem, not a convenience. Whoever holds the password can change your recovery details, lock you out, and stand between you and your own money. Our line is co-manager access only, never passwords — and you should hold every agency, us included, to it.

## Ask what they are guaranteeing you

This is close to a trick question, and a good agency passes it by refusing the bait. Ask what they guarantee. The honest answer is that no one can promise a specific income, and no one can promise the platform will never act against your account — enforcement is outside every agency's control.

A straight answer owns that plainly. A guaranteed result, or a promise you will never be banned, is not confidence; it is a claim no one can keep, and it tells you how this agency treats every other promise it makes. Treat a guarantee as a reason to leave the room, not a selling point. The agencies worth trusting are careful about what they say they can control, because they know the difference.

## Ask them to show you the business

Ask to see the company: the legal entity, the address, the people. A real agency is a checkable business — a registered entity you can find in a state's records, a real business address, named humans who stand behind it, and email from its own domain rather than a free inbox. How to run that check is its own guide: [verifying a real business](/resources/agency-selection/verifying-a-real-business).

A straight answer hands it over without drama. A dodge is "we keep that private," an anonymous brand with no names, or an operation reachable only through a personal messaging app. For the record, so you can practise the check on us: we are Sinaura Collectives LLC, a New Jersey company, reachable at contact@sinauraco.com. Look us up, and hold everyone you talk to — us included — to being a business you can actually find.

## Ask whether you even need an agency yet

Save one question for them, and one for yourself: do I need an agency at my stage, or would software and my own effort serve me better right now? An honest agency will tell you when the answer is "not yet" rather than sign you anyway. We lay out the trade-off in [agency vs software](/resources/agency-selection/agency-vs-software).

A straight answer is stage-dependent and unafraid to point you elsewhere: management earns its share when there is enough volume and operational load that a team beats what you can do alone. A dodge is "every creator needs an agency" — a line that describes their incentive, not your situation. An agency willing to talk you out of signing when it is not the right call is showing you the judgment you would be hiring.
